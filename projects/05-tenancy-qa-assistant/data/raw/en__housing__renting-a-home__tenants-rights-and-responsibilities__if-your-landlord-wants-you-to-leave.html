Rules for ending a tenancy

Landlords must follow certain rules if they want you to leave your rented accommodation.

The rules apply to:

  • Private landlords
  • Approved housing bodies
  • People who let student-specific accommodation

If your landlord wants to end your tenancy, they must:

  • Serve you with a notice of termination
  • Give you the correct amount of notice
  • Send a copy of the notice of termination to the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB)

If your tenancy has lasted less than 6 months, your landlord can ask you to leave without giving you a reason.

If your tenancy has lasted more than 6 months, your landlord must give you a valid reason why they are terminating your tenancy.

The valid reasons are different depending on if your tenancy began:

  • On or after 1 March 2026
  • Before 1 March 2026

Note: If you have a fixed-term tenancy, your landlord cannot end the tenancy before the end of the fixed term unless you breach your obligations.

Valid reasons for ending a tenancy created from 1 March 2026

If your tenancy was created on or after 1 March 2026, you have a tenancy of minimum duration when you have rented for 6 months without getting a notice of termination.

A tenancy of minimum duration runs for 6-year cycles, which means you have security of tenure for 6 years at a time.

Your landlord can only end a tenancy of minimum duration for certain reasons. These reasons depend on how many tenancies your landlord has.

  • Larger landlords are registered companies, or landlords with 4 or more tenancies.
  • Smaller landlords are landlords that have 3 or fewer tenancies.

Registered companies are classified as larger landlords, even if they have 3 or less tenancies.

Reasons for larger landlords to end a tenancy of minimum duration

Larger landlords can only end a tenancy of minimum duration if:

  • The tenants are not meeting their obligations, such as paying rent
  • The property is no longer suitable, for example, the property is now too small for a family or is not accessible

Larger landlords must follow the correct process when ending a tenancy for these reasons. This includes issuing a valid notice of termination and meeting any other requirements.

Reasons for smaller landlords to end a tenancy of minimum duration

Smaller landlords can only end a tenancy during the 6-year period of a TMD if:

  • The tenant is not meeting their obligations, such as paying the rent
  • The property is no longer suitable for the tenant, for example, the property is now too small for a family or is not accessible
  • The landlord is experiencing hardship, for example, because of a separation, homelessness, bankruptcy or after returning to Ireland from abroad
  • The landlord needs the property for themselves or an immediate family member to live in. In this situation immediate family members can be, the landlord's spouse, civil partner, child, stepchild, foster child, adopted child, parent, step-parent or parent in-law.

After the 6-year period, smaller landlords can also end a tenancy of minimum duration for these other reasons:

  • They intend to sell the property
  • They are substantially refurbishing or renovating the property
  • They need the property for themselves or a family member to use. In this situation, a family member can be the landlord's spouse, civil partner, child, stepchild, foster child, adopted child, parent, step-parent or parent in-law, grandchild, grandparent, brother, sister, nephew or niece.
  • They are changing the use of the property

Small landlords must follow the correct process when ending a tenancy for these reasons. This includes issuing a valid notice of termination and meeting any other requirements.

Valid reasons for ending a tenancy created before 1 March 2026

If your tenancy was created before 1 March 2026, your landlord can only end a tenancy after the first 6 months if:

  • You do not comply with the obligations of the tenancy, for example, by not paying your rent on time
  • The property is no longer suited to your needs, for example, if it is too small
  • The landlord intends to sell the property within 9 months. However, this may not apply if the landlord plans to sell 10 or more dwellings in a development within a 6-month period – see ‘Restriction on terminating when selling multiple properties’ below
  • The landlord needs the property for their own use or for an immediate family member (this only applies to private landlords)
  • The landlord plans to change the use of the property (for example, convert it from residential use to office use)
  • The landlord intends to refurbish the property substantially

Additional reasons if your tenancy started before 11 June 2022

If your tenancy was created before 11 June 2022, your landlord can also terminate your tenancy for any reason at the end of your 6-year, Part 4 tenancy. Your landlord must:

  • Serve a notice of termination before the end of the 6 years, and
  • Give a notice period that expires on or after the end of the tenancy

If the tenancy is not ended after 6 years, a new tenancy will begin. This new tenancy will be a tenancy of unlimited duration and this exception will no longer apply.

By June 2028, all Part 4 tenancies will be tenancies of unlimited duration, and the above exception will no longer apply to them. This is because all existing 6-year cycle tenancies will have ended by then.

Your landlord must follow the correct process when ending a tenancy for these reasons.

Restriction on terminating tenancies when selling multiple properties

A landlord cannot terminate a tenancy of more than 6 months if they intend to sell 10 or more homes in a development within a 6-month period, unless:

  • They can show that the market value with a sitting tenant is over 20% below the market value with vacant possession, and
  • Preventing them from terminating the tenancy would be unduly onerous on them or would cause them undue hardship

This provision is often called the ‘Tyrrelstown amendment’.

Notice of termination

If your landlord wants you to leave, they must serve you with a valid written notice of termination.

The law sets out the requirements for a valid notice of termination. It must:

  • Be in writing
  • Be signed by the landlord, or their authorised agent
  • Specify the date when the tenancy will end
  • State that you have the whole 24 hours of the termination date to vacate the property
  • Specify the date of the notice itself. This is the date the notice is hand delivered, emailed, or left at the tenant's address.
  • Give the reason for ending the tenancy, if a tenancy has lasted more than 6 months or is a fixed-term tenancy
  • State that any issue about the validity of the notice or the right of the landlord to serve it must be referred to the RTB within 90 days from getting the notice. (This 90-day timeframe applies unless the tenant is not keeping their obligations.)
  • Be sent to the tenant and the RTB. The landlord must send a copy of the notice to the RTB within 7 days of when they serve the notice to their tenant.

Since 14 September 2026, a landlord has 7 days from the date the tenant was served the notice of termination to send a copy to the RTB. Previously, they had to send the copy to the RTB at the same time.

How a notice of termination should be sent

A notice of termination can be:

  • Sent to you by email
  • Given to you in person
  • Left at your home

Sending a notice of termination to the RTB

Since 14 September 2026, your landlord has 7 days from when you get the notice of termination to send a copy to the RTB.

Landlords should no longer post notices of termination to tenants. They should email, hand-deliver, or leave the notice at their tenant’s address. This ensures that the tenant gets the notice of termination on the date listed on the notice.

A notice of termination can be sent to the RTB by:

  • Uploading it to the RTB Service Centre
  • Emailing to NoticeofTermination@rtb.ie
  • Posting it to the RTB

Other requirements for landlords ending a tenancy

Your landlord must send you a valid notice of termination when ending your tenancy.

If they are ending the tenancy for certain reasons, they may also need to give you:

  • The right to return to the property, if the property comes up for rent again in a certain timeframe
  • Additional documents, such as statutory declarations, statements or warning notices

Requirement to offer the property back to the tenant

Your landlord must give you the right to move back into the property, if your tenancy was ended for certain reasons and the property becomes available to rent again after a set amount of time.

The table below shows why and when your landlord must offer the property back to you based on the reason your tenancy ended.

What is a statutory declaration?

A statutory declaration is a signed statement that information is true or accurate. In Ireland, you sign a statutory declaration in the presence of a:

  • Commissioner for Oaths
  • Practicing solicitor
  • Notary public
  • Peace commissioner

There are more statutory declarations available for joint landlords. You can find these on the RTB’s website.

Additional rules if your tenancy is ending because a breach of tenant obligations

If your landlord is terminating your tenancy because you have broken your tenant obligations, you must get a written warning notice at least 28 days before the notice of termination is issued.

However, if your behaviour is seriously anti-social your landlord does not have to give you a warning notice. The Notice periods section below has more information on this process.

Making a complaint about a notice of termination

You can complain to the Residential Tenancies Board if you think:

  • Your notice of termination is invalid
  • The reason your landlord gives for ending the tenancy is not allowed

Minor errors in a notice of termination

When dealing with a dispute about a notice of termination, the RTB can overlook a minor error or omission in a notice of termination.

They can do this if the error does not prejudice the notice of termination and the notice would otherwise be valid. This is known as the ‘slip rule’.

A ‘remedial notice’ can be issued that fixes the error with the original notice. For more information on remedial notices see the RTB’s website.

Since 14 September 2026, the slip rule also applies to any additional documents you might need for a notice of termination, such as a statutory declaration or statement. This means that minor errors in these documents can also be overlooked and corrected.

What are the notice periods?

The length of notice required depends on the length of your tenancy.

Length of tenancy Notice that the landlord must give
Less than 6 months At least 90 days up to a maximum of 120 days
6 months or longer but less than 1 year 152 days
1 year or longer but less than 7 years 180 days
7 years or longer but less than 8 years 196 days
More than 8 years 224 days

These notice periods have applied since 6 July 2022, when changes under the Regulation of Providers of Building Works and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2022 took effect.

Exceptions to required notice periods

Not keeping your tenant obligations

If you are not keeping your obligations, your landlord must send you a warning notice before they can issue a notice of termination.

The warning notice must outline what obligations have been broken and give you a reasonable amount of time to fix the issue. This warning notice needs to be copied to the RTB.

If you do not resolve the issue, the landlord only needs to give you 28 days’ notice of termination. This applies to all tenancies, regardless of the length of the tenancy.

However, if your behaviour is seriously anti-social or threatens the fabric of the property, the landlord only needs to give you 7 days’ notice and does not have to give you a warning notice.

The Residential Tenancies Act 2004 sets out the type of seriously anti-social behaviour for which a 7-day notice may be allowed.

Rent arrears

If you haven’t paid your rent, your landlord must send you a rent arrears warning notice before they can issue a notice of termination.

The rent arrears warning notice must state the amount of rent you owe and give you 28 days to pay it. The landlord must send a copy of the rent arrears warning notice to the RTB.

The 28 days you have to pay the rent arrears counts from the date you and the RTB get the warning notice. If your landlord does not send the warning notice to you and the RTB, a notice of termination will not be valid.

If you do not pay the rent arrears in the 28 days, your landlord can then give you 28 days’ notice of termination. The landlord must send the notice of termination to the RTB on the same day it is sent to you. If this is not done the notice of termination will be invalid.

This process for ending a tenancy because of rent arrears is set out in Section 12 of the Residential Tenancies and Valuation Act 2020.

The RTB has information about this rent arrears process.

Can a landlord and tenant agree a shorter notice period?

You and your landlord can agree a shorter notice period than the minimum periods set out above. But you can only do this when the landlord decides to end your tenancy.

So, for example, if you find somewhere else to live before the termination date on your notice of termination, you should write to your landlord confirming the date you will be leaving. This can help avoid any disputes in the future. You should check the terms of your lease to see if there are any other requirements around ending your tenancy, particularly if you have a fixed-term tenancy.

It is illegal to agree a shorter notice period at the start of the tenancy.

You and your landlord can also agree a longer notice period, but the maximum is 120 days when the tenancy has lasted less than 6 months.

What if I can’t find alternative accommodation?

If your landlord has asked you to leave and you can’t find alternative accommodation, you can contact organisations such as Threshold or Focus Ireland for advice. They can check if your notice of termination is valid and help you with the next steps.

If you are illegally evicted or are unable to leave your rented accommodation after your tenancy has been terminated, you can find information in our page about illegal eviction and overholding.

If you find yourself homeless or you think you may lose your home, you should contact your local authority to access accommodation. There are supports available for people who are homeless and agencies that can help.

More information

The Residential Tenancies Board has more information about ending a tenancy.

Threshold can also advise you, if you need help checking if you notice of termination is valid.

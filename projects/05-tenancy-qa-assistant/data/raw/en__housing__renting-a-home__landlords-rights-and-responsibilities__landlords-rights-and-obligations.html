Private landlords' rights, responsibilities and the law

If you own land or a building and rent it to a tenant, you are considered a landlord.

Your main rights and responsibilities as a landlord come from:

  • Landlord and tenant law
  • The lease or tenancy agreement (written or spoken) you have with your tenant

Landlord and tenant law

Residential tenancies legislation outlines most landlord’s rights and obligations. You can read a consolidated version of the Residential Tenancies Act 2004 on the Law Reform Commission's website.

Leases and tenancy agreements

Leases or other tenancy agreements cannot take away from your landlord or tenant rights and responsibilities in the legislation.

However, you and your tenant can agree on things that are not covered by legislation in a lease or tenancy agreement, for example, who pays for the utility bills.

If you are renting a room in your home, the tenancy does not come under the residential tenancies legislation. However, you are covered if you are renting out a self-contained flat in your home. Read more in our page Renting out a room in your home.

Approved housing body landlords and the law

Approved housing bodies (AHBs) are covered by residential tenancies legislation. AHB landlords have most of the same rights and responsibilities as private landlords.

However, there are some differences, including the rules on rent reviews, minimum standards and a landlord’s right to end a tenancy.

Student-specific accommodation landlords and the law

Student-specific accommodation (SSA) is housing that is:

  • Built or designated for students
  • Used solely to provide residential accommodation to students during the academic term

Landlords and tenants in student-specific accommodation are protected in residential tenancies legislation. This means that they have most of the same rights as private landlords and tenants. However, there are some differences:

  • Tenants in student-specific accommodation do not have security of tenure
  • Landlords may be able to access communal areas without the permission of the tenants
  • Tenancy or licence arrangements in student-specific accommodation should not be more than 41 weeks.
  • Tenants in student-specific accommodation only need to give you 28 days’ notice when they end their tenancy. You must give them more notice when ending their tenancy.
  • Your tenant can terminate a fixed-term tenancy any time between 1 May and 1 October each year for any reason. But, outside of these dates, the existing rules about ending a fixed-term tenancy apply and they can only end the tenancy for specific reasons.
  • The rules for setting and reviewing the rent are different

Find out more about student-specific accommodation on the RTB’s website.

Your rights as a landlord

As a landlord, you have the right to:

  • Set the rent
  • Get the correct rent on the date it is due
  • Get any charges associated with the property (this means taxes and duties or payments)
  • Terminate a tenancy during the first 6 months without giving a reason
  • Terminate a tenancy at a later stage in certain circumstances
  • Be told about who lives at the property (this does not include overnight visitors or short stays)
  • Be told about any repairs needed
  • Be given reasonable access to the property to carry out repairs
  • Carry out routine inspections at a mutually agreed time. Repairs or inspections should be arranged in advance with your tenant, unless there is an emergency. If you are selling the property, your tenant must allow you access for viewings, but this must be arranged with them beforehand.
  • Refer disputes to the RTB – but only if you have registered the tenancy
  • Review the rent. There are rules about how often you can review the rent in a private tenancy. AHB rents are reviewed every 12 months, or according to the tenancy agreement.
  • Decide if the tenant in a private rented tenancy can sub-let or assign a tenancy. However, if you refuse to allow a tenant to assign or sublet a tenancy, this can give the tenant the right to terminate a fixed-term tenancy before it expires.

You do not have the right to:

  • Enter your tenant's home without permission
  • Take or keep your tenant’s property – even if they haven’t paid the rent
  • Charge more than the market rate for the property
  • Penalise your tenant for bringing a dispute to the RTB

Your responsibilities as a landlord

As a landlord, you must:

  • Register the tenancy every year with the RTB and update them about any changes to the tenancy, such as a change in rent
  • Give your tenant a rent book or statement of rent paid
  • Make sure that the property meets certain minimum standards. The standards for food preparation, storage and laundry purposes do not apply to AHBs.
  • Repair and maintain the inside of the property to the standard it was in at the start of the tenancy
  • Repair and maintain the structure of the property
  • Provide a Building Energy Rating (BER) for the property
  • Pay your tenants back for any repairs they make which are your responsibility
  • Insure the property. If it is impossible to get insurance, or if the cost is unreasonable, this does not apply.
  • Give your tenant information about any agents who are authorised to deal with them on your behalf. For example, management companies, agencies, personal representatives.
  • Make sure that your tenant knows how to contact you or your agent
  • Give your private tenant 90 days’ notice of a rent review and follow the rules about how often you can do this. AHBs should give notice as soon as is practicable.
  • Give your tenants a valid written notice of termination and follow all the rules around terminating a tenancy

You must also make sure that your tenants meet their obligations. Anyone who is affected by your tenants’ failure to meet their obligations can make a complaint against you to the RTB.

Advertising accommodation for sexual activity

You cannot offer or advertise accommodation in exchange for sexual activity. This is a criminal offence that applies to tenancies and licence arrangements and can result in Class A fine of up to €5,000. This is set out in the Criminal Law, Civil Law and Defence (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2026.

Deposits

You cannot ask for upfront payments of more than 2 month’s rent when renting out a property. This includes a deposit of a month’s rent and one month’s rent in advance.

Students in student-specific tenancies can opt-out of this restriction and pay a larger upfront payment if they want. However, this opt-out is only allowed if the student is paying rent and tuition to the same provider, such as a public or private educational provider.

You can only withhold a tenant’s deposit if:

  • Your tenant has not given you proper notice when ending the tenancy
  • You have been left with unpaid rent or outstanding bills, for example for electricity or gas
  • Your tenant has caused damage beyond normal wear and tear

The Residential Tenancies (Amendment) Act 2015 provides for a tenancy deposit protection scheme, where the RTB would manage and hold deposits for tenants and landlords. These provisions are not yet in effect.

Refusing a tenancy

Landlords cannot discriminate against potential tenants on grounds of gender, civil status, family status, sexual orientation, religion, age, disability, race or membership of the Traveller community.

You cannot discriminate against a tenant or potential tenant because they are getting a Housing Assistance Payment (HAP), Rent Supplement or any other social welfare payment.

This means that you cannot state when advertising accommodation that HAP or Rent Supplement is not accepted and you cannot refuse to rent accommodation to someone because they are getting a social welfare payment.

The is outlined in the Equal Status Acts, which apply to lettings and accommodation.

Read more on the Irish Human Rights and Equality Commission's website.

Ending a tenancy

If you want to end a tenancy, you must serve your tenant with a valid written Notice of Termination and give them the correct amount of notice.

You must send a copy of this Notice of Termination to the RTB. Since 14 September 2026, you have 7 days from the date the tenant is served the notice of termination to send a copy to the RTB.

You can find out more on ending a tenancy the RTB's website.

What if I break these rules?

Landlords who break their obligations can be dealt with in several ways.

You can be:

  • Prosecuted
  • Investigated and sanctioned
  • Fined

Criminal prosecution

You can be criminally prosecuted for breaking certain rental rules, such as not registering a tenancy or not complying with a determination order. This can result in:

  • A criminal conviction
  • A fine of up to €5,000
  • Imprisonment for up to 12 months

Investigation and sanction

The RTB can investigate, caution and sanction you if you break your statutory obligations as a landlord.

The RTB can investigate several issues, such as not registering a tenancy or following the rent increase rules.

Find out exactly what issues the RTB can investigate in our page Reporting an issue to the RTB.

If you are found guilty any of breaking these rules, you can get:

  • A written caution
  • A fine of up to €15,000 for each breach
  • Additional costs up to €15,000 for each breach

The RTB has more information about decisions and sanctions.

Fixed payment fines

Since 14 September 2026, the RTB can issue fixed payment fines if you break certain rental laws, such as:

  • Not registering a tenancy
  • Not updating details about a tenancy
  • Not giving rent setting information at the start of a tenancy
  • Setting the rent above the rent increase limits
  • Not notifying the RTB of a rent exemption
  • Not providing a rent review notice correctly

You can be fined €200 for breaking the rent increase rules and €100 for breaking the registration rules.

You have 28 days from the date of the notice to pay the fine. If you do not to pay the fine during this time, you may be prosecuted.

As well as issuing these fines, the RTB can also prosecute and investigate.

You can find out more about these fines on the RTB’s website.

Rental income and tax

Tax issues

Landlords pay tax on rental income.

You can claim certain expenses against your rental income to reduce the amount of tax you pay. For example, you can claim for repairs to the property and management fees.

Revenue has a list if what expenses you are allowed to deduct when paying tax on your rental income.

You declare your rental income in your annual tax return, using either Revenue's myAccount Service or ROS (Revenue Online Service). Revenue has more information on how you can do this.

Mortgage interest

You can deduct the interest on mortgages used to purchase, improve or repair rented property when working out your rental income for tax purposes.

You must show that you have registered all tenancies in the property with the RTB. You can deduct 100% of the interest on these mortgages.

Interest is treated as accruing on a daily basis and the date the loan was taken out is not relevant. Interest can only be deducted during the period in which the property is let.

Rent a room relief

If you rent out a room or flat in your home, you are exempt from income tax on the amount that your tenant pays you for rent and other services, up to €14,000 in a tax year. This exemption does not apply to income you get from renting your property for short-term lets.

Rental income tax relief

A temporary rental income tax relief was introduced in 2024 to support private landlords. The Residential Premises Rental Income Relief (RPRIR) is available against some rental income at the standard rate of 20% each year. It is from 2024 to 2027 and only applies if you keep the rental property in the rental market for the next 4 years. You can get 20% of:

  • €3,000 for 2024
  • €4,000 for 2025
  • €5,000 for 2026 and 2027

The relief reduces the tax due on rental income by up to €600 in 2024, €800 in 2025, and €1,000 in 2026 and 2027. The relief will be clawed back if you leave the rental market during this time.

Revenue has more information about the Residential Premises Rental Income Relief.

Paying tax on rental income, if you are a landlord living abroad

If you are living outside Ireland and renting out a property in Ireland, you still need to declare your rental income to Revenue and pay tax on it. You can do this by:

  • Getting your tenant to withhold 20% of their gross rent and pay it to Revenue
  • Use a collection agent to withhold 20% of your tenant’s rent to pay it to Revenue

Read more in our page on tax relief and other issues for people renting.

Private tenancies and receivership

If the mortgage on your property is in arrears and the mortgage lender has appointed a receiver, your tenants must pay the rent to the receiver. However, you remain legally responsible for things such as returning the tenants’ deposits. The receiver may arrange to do repairs.

Read more in the Residential Tenant’s Guide to Receivership (pdf) published by Banking and Payments Federation Ireland.
